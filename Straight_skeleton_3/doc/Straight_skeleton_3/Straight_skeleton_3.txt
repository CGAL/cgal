\section Straight_skeleton_3Introduction Introduction

This package implements weighted straight skeletons of three-dimensional polyhedra.
The approach is a direct extension of the method is used in the package
\ref PkgStraightSkeleton2Ref "2D Straight Skeleton".

\subsection Straight_skeleton_3Straight Straight Skeletons of Polyhedra

An intuitive way to think of the construction of straight skeletons is to imagine that wavefronts
(or grassfires) are spawned at each face of the polyhedron, and are moving inward, parallel
to the face. As the fronts progress, vertices and edges of the polyhedron shift
along the angular bisector of the planes subtending their incident faces,
and the shape of faces change depending on the angles formed between polyhedral faces,
and on the speeds assigned to each front.

</center>

from left to right). The input is in gray, and the shifts for two values are drawn in shades of blue.
Vertices move along angular trisectors and edges along angular bisectors (in red).
\cgalFigureCaptionEnd

During the transformation, the topology of the polyhedron will generally change such as a vertex
hitting a face and piercing it, or an edge collapsing.
The trace of the shifting polyhedral elements under this transformation forms
a complex structure: the <em>straight skeleton</em>.

\section Straight_skeleton_3Definitions Definitions

We now give precise definitions of the various concepts relevant to this package.

\subsection Straight_skeleton_3PolyhedronDef Input Polyhedron

The definition of polyhedron used hereafter is standard and in line with what can be found
in the rest of \cgal, for example in \ref PkgSurfaceMesh, \ref PkgPolyhedron,
or \ref PkgPolygonMeshProcessing.

We first define a <em>convex domain</em> as a bounded, non-empty intersection of a finite
number of halfspaces of the Euclidean three-space \f$ \mathbb{R}^3\f$. A <em>domain</em> is then
a bounded subset of \f$ \mathbb{R}^3\f$ which can be expressed as the finite union of convex domains.
A domain can thus have holes, handles, disconnected connected components, etc.

The boundary of a domain can be represented as a structural complex, which consists of vertices,
edges, faces, and symmetric incidence relations between these elements \cgalCite{k-ugpdd-99}.
Such combinatorial entity is called a polyhedron. Its vertices embed into \f$ \mathbb{R}^3\f$ as 3D points;
its edges as straight line segments between two points; and faces as planar, possibly non-convex
polygons (possibly with holes).

With the definition above, a polyhedron forms 2-manifold structure in a combinatorial sense.
In our context, we furthermore append to this definition that it must also be <em>geometrically manifold</em>:
there should not be so-called touching elements such as a vertex or an edge touching the
interior of a non-incident face. Formally, the geometric intersection of two non-incident elements
of the polyhedron must be empty, and the intersection of two incident elements must be exactly
their common (topological) boundary.

\subsection Straight_skeleton_3StraightSkeletonDef Straight Skeleton

We call the structural elements of an input polyhedron <em>contour elements</em>.

\note A polyhedron is a surface structure whose two-dimensional elements are generally called <em> faces</em>.
The 3D straight skeleton, despite also being a 2-dimensional structure embedded in 3D space,
induces naturally a division of space into 3D cells. As a three-dimensional structure usually
use the term <em>facet</em> for two-dimensional elements, we shall refer to polyhedron faces
as contour <em>facets</em>, for consistency.

Given a contour facet called the <em>source facet</em>, its <em>offset facet</em> at time `t` is a facet
parallel and with the same orientation and such that the Euclidean distance between the planes
supporting both facets is exactly `t`. In mathematical terms, if the supporting plane of the source facet
is described through the equation `ax+by+cz+d=0`, then the supporting plane of the offset facet
at a time `t` is `ax+by+cz+d-t=0`. In our setting, an offset facet shall always be located on the bounded
side of the polyhedron.

The straight skeleton is constructed by applying a continuous, <I>inward offsetting</I> to the contour facets.
We also call this process <em>polyhedron facet offsetting</em>, or simply <em>polyhedron offsetting</em>.
Under this transformation, contour vertices (edges) move along the angular trisector (bisector)
of the planes subtending their incident facets, at a speed that depends on the angle between
the three (two) incident contour facets. As elements shift, topological change may happen,
such as two moving vertices impacting each other, or a moving vertex colliding with a moving facet.
We call these topological changes <em>events</em>. When an event happens, the combinatorial structure
of the polyhedron must be modified to retain the validity of the polyhedron during future offsetting.

</center>
\cgalFigureCaptionBegin{SPS3PierceEvent}

The polyhedron's combinatorial structure must be updated to represent the newly created hole.
\cgalFigureCaptionEnd

The straight skeleton is composed of three types of elements: nodes, spokes, and sheets.
They describe the geometry traced by the moving elements of the input polyhedron:

- A <em>node</em> is a 0-dimensional entity. It is either a contour vertex or corresponds to an event.
  It embeds into \f$ \mathbb{R}^3\f$ as a 3D point.
- A <em>spoke</em> is a 1-dimensional entity, linking two nodes. It embeds into \f$ \mathbb{R}^3\f$
  as a straight line segment, the trace of a moving polyhedron vertex during offsetting.
  The supporting line of a spoke is the angular trisector of the three planes supporting the incident
  contour facets.
- A <em>sheet</em> is a 2-dimensional entity, a polygonal domain bounded by spokes and contour edges.
  It is the trace of a moving polyhedron edge during offsetting. A sheet lives on the bisector plane
  of the two planes supporting the incident contour facets.

The straight skeleton forms a unique partition of the interior of the polyhedron into <I>straight skeleton regions</I>,
corresponding to the monotone areas traced by the continuous inward offsetting of the contour facets.
Each region corresponds uniquely to one contour facet, which is a facet of that region.
Regions are bounded by the angular bisectors of the supporting planes of the contour facets,
and each such region is itself a connected polyhedron, as defined in Section \ref Straight_skeleton_3PolyhedronDef.

\cgalFigureAnchor{SPS3Nomenclature}

</center>

\cgalFigureCaptionEnd

Constructing the <em>exterior</em> skeleton of a polyhedron is achieved by reversing the orientation
of the contour facets: inwards shifting becomes outwards with respect to the original polyhedron.
In an exterior skeleton, some spokes and sheets are unbounded. Nonetheless, since the number of possible
events is bounded, there exists a time after which there are no events and the complete skeleton
structure is known and stable. In practice, the exterior skeleton is computed at a user-defined maximum
time, meaning that nodes (events) that would be created after that time are not computed, and spokes
and sheets may be fully present, absent, or truncated.

\subsection Straight_skeleton_3WeightedStraightSkeletonDef Weighted Straight Skeleton

Weighted straight skeletons generalize standard straight skeletons by assigning a weight to each
contour facet. The weight may be understood as the propagation speed of the corresponding wavefront.
In weighted straight skeletons, vertices (edges) no longer move along the angular trisectors (bisectors)
between three (two) contour facets, but along the <em>weighted</em> trisector (bisector).

This \cgal package supports positive multiplicative weights: if the supporting plane of a contour facet
is described through the equation `ax+by+cz+d=0`, and the supporting plane of the offset facet
at distance `t` is `ax+by+cz+d-t=0` then with a multiplicative weight `w`, the equation becomes
`ax+by+cz+d-wt=0`. Therefore, a larger weight encodes a faster moving front.

\cgalFigureAnchor{SPS3Weight}
<center>
<img src="sps_weight.png" style="max-width:100%;"/>
</center>
\cgalFigureCaptionBegin{SPS3Weight}
Straight skeletons obtained with uniform weights (left) and with different weights for horizontal
and vertical facets (right).
\cgalFigureCaptionEnd

A significant change from unweighted straight skeletons of polyhedra is that the sheets of a weighted
straight skeleton of a polyhedron with holes are no longer necessarily simply connected surfaces:
for example, a sheet can completely encompass a set of sheets incident to a hole, resulting in the
topology of a ball with a void.

\section Straight_skeleton_3PolyhedronOffsetting Polyhedron Offsetting

The straight skeleton is defined by the trace of moving polyhedral elements, but the polyhedron
surface itself during translation (and modification) of its faces is itself of interest.
We call the surface modified by events up till time `t` the <I>offset polyhedron</I> at time `t`.

An offset polyhedron can have fewer, the same number of, or more faces than its source polyhedron;
it can even consist of multiple connected components. If the source polyhedron is hole-free, its offsets are
also hole-free. However, if the source polyhedron has holes, an offset polyhedron may also contain
holes, or it may have none at all (e.g., if the offset distance is sufficiently large).

Outward offsetting, similarly to exterior straight skeletons, is achieved by reversing
the orientation of the faces of the input polyhedron.

\section Straight_skeleton_32D3DComparison Comparison with Two-Dimensional Straight Skeleton Construction

Although the definition of a straight skeleton of a polyhedron is identical in both 2D and 3D,
there exist some major differences between the two settings.

A first difference is immediately visible as one starts shifting facets in 3D: where a vertex of
a 2D polygon has only 2 incident edges, a vertex in 3D can have an arbitrary number of incident edges
and facets. If the number of edges incident to a vertex is greater than 3, we call the vertex a
<em>high-degree</em> vertex. In 2D, a vertex remains well defined as edges shift: its position is
the intersection of the supporting lines of the translated edges. (Even in the degenerate case of
parallel edges, the trace of the moving vertex is still well defined as the line orthogonal
to the supporting line, and passing through a parent vertex.)

On the contrary, when the 3D facets incident to a high-degree vertex shift, they often no longer intersect
in a single point simply because in general setting there is no reason for four (or more) planes
to intersect in a single point. Instead, a high-degree vertex will generally split under any shift
into a tree (graph) of degree 3 vertices, whose embedding is free of self-intersections. Furthermore,
there may be multiple valid embeddings after such a split. Consequently, a fundamental difference
is that the straight skeleton is not uniquely defined in 3D.

\cgalFigureAnchor{SPS3VertexSplit}
<center>
<img src="sps_vertex_split.png" style="max-width:100%;"/>
</center>
\cgalFigureCaptionBegin{SPS3VertexSplit}
Splitting a high-degree vertex.
\cgalFigureCaptionEnd

The ambiguity stemming from the arbitrary choice of vertex split can also arise during event handling:
when an edge collapses, facet propagation may yield two valid geometric embeddings, requiring
another arbitrary choice.

\cgalFigureAnchor{SPS3EdgeCollapseAmbiguity}
<center>
<img src="sps_event_edge_flip.png" style="max-width:100%;"/>
</center>
\cgalFigureCaptionBegin{SPS3EdgeCollapseAmbiguity}
When handling the event of an edge collapsing, there may be multiple valid treatments.
\cgalFigureCaptionEnd

With degenerate inputs, the split of a high-degree vertex becomes much more complex: firstly,
some of the vertices spawned by a shift may remain high-degree, that is the output is not necessarily
a tree of degree 3 vertices. Worse, the graph of vertices after any arbitrarily small shift
may no longer form a single connected component (i.e., it is a forest), as depicted in the figure
below.

\cgalFigureAnchor{SPS3VertexSplitForest}
<center>
<img src="sps_forest.png" style="max-width:100%;"/>
</center>
\cgalFigureCaptionBegin{SPS3VertexSplitForest}
In degenerate configurations, the split of a high-degree vertex can be arbitrarily complex,
even creating multiple disconnected edge graphs (Figure from \cgalCite{cgal:b-mbras-10}).
\cgalFigureCaptionEnd

Finally, the main difference, which results from the previous vertex behavior observations,
is that possible topological changes for an arbitrary polyhedron input can be arbitrarily complex:
faces may partially merge together, vertices may pierce in the direction of an edge, etc.
Some complex changes can also happen in 2D, for example partial edge merges of polygons,
but such degenerate events can be decomposed into a series of simple events, followed by a geometric
merge of identical positions, and an untangling of the skeleton. In 3D, a similar approach is, at
least for this package, out-of-reach.

Consequently, this package imposes restrictions on the nature of input polyhedra to simplify the problem,
as described in the following section.

\section Straight_skeleton_3GeneralPosition The "General Position" Precondition

To reduce the complexity of the problem of generating offset polyhedra through 3D straight skeletons,
we impose a significant constraint on the valid polyhedra that can be handled by this package:
in addition to the usual requirements described in Section \ref Straight_skeleton_3PolyhedronDef,
we require that the polyhedron must be in <em>general position</em>: all combinations of three planes
must intersect in a unique point.

Under this strong assumption, it is proven that one can decompose the construction of a 3D straight
skeleton in a succcession of events classified in 13 different event types. These events are direct
extensions of the 2D straight skeleton events, and can be regrouped in two categories:
<em>vanish events</em> and <em>contact events</em>. Vanish events are local combinatorial changes
stemming from the collapse of edges (thus akin to the edge event in 2D); contact events are interaction
with non-local elements of the mesh such as a vertex piercing a face (thus akin to split events in 2D).
We refer to \cgalCite{cgal:w-sspp-15} and \cgalCite{cgal:aw-ssmonp-16} for the complete description.

\cgalFigureAnchor{SPS3Events}
<center>
<img src="sps_events.png" style="max-width:100%;"/>
</center>
\cgalFigureCaptionBegin{SPS3Events}
Three of the thirteen possible events: an edge event (left), a triangle event (middle), and an
edge split event (right).
\cgalFigureCaptionEnd

In addition, under the hypothesis of general position, the topologies of the polyhedron
and straight skeleton become simpler: vertices of an (offset) polyhedron have degree 3,
each spoke is incident to exactly three sheets, and each node is incident to four spokes and four sheets.

\subsection Straight_skeleton_3GeneralPositionInPractice In Practice: Input Perturbation

The hypothesis stipulated in the previous section makes it possible to use an event-based approach,
but in practice very few polyhedra actually satisfy such a demanding requirement.
For example, an input as simple as the unit cube is not in general position (parallel faces).
Most real-world polyhedra also have high-degree vertices, which automatically make the input violate
the hypothesis since by definition this means N plans going through a point.

To make this package still usable with non-trivial models, a pre-processing step is performed
with the goal of constructing a polyhedron that is in general position and that is arbitrarily close
to the input polyhedron. This step is called the <em> perturbation</em> step.
The perturbation step is essentially composed of two stages: first, it performs a tiny,
random nudge of the plane coefficients; secondly, it recovers an embedding of the polyhedron
by reconstructing a valid surface at all high-degree vertices, splitting them into degree 3 vertices.

The magnitude of said nudge of course impacts the closeness to the input. By default,
the perturbation value is chosen very small (1e-7 times the local coordinate magnitude), with
the idea that the effect is thus invisible as it becomes diluted in the numerical errors
that arise while performing inexact computations, or during input/output manipulations
(which pratically always using limited precision).
The magnitude of the perturbation can be adjusted by users such that it is below a meaningfulness
threshold with respect to their data. There is of course a downside to using infinitesimal nudges as
computations become slower and, like most exact computation algorithms, the embedding back
into real-world coordinates could suffer from self-intersections if there is not enough
room in the floating point world to embed back exact numbers.

The goal of this package is thus to provide valid results that are arbitrarily close to the correct result.

\section Straight_skeleton_3Implementation Implementation

The algorithm implemented in this package is an extension of the method used
in \ref PkgStraightSkeleton2Ref "2D Straight Skeleton": modifications of the combinatorial structure
of the polyhedron, called <em>events</em>, are detected and treated in a chronological order.

\subsection Straight_skeleton_3API API

Contrary to the 2D, the extraction of an offset polyhedron at a given time `t` is more complex
than drawing an isoline at height `t` of an existing straight skeleton. As such,
functions of this package compute the straight skeleton <em>and</em> offset polyhedra
simultaneously.

The main function to construct offset polyhedra is `CGAL::create_straight_skeleton_and_offset_polyhedra_3()`.
If offset polyhedra are not of interest, one may simply call `CGAL::create_straight_skeleton_3()`,
which merely builds and returns the straight skeleton structure, using the class `CGAL::Straight_skeleton_3`.

\subsection Straight_skeleton_3TraitsChoice Kernel Choice

The current algorithm requires a kernel providing <em>at least</em> exact predicates
and exact constructions, possibly with exact square roots. Users should by default use
`CGAL::Exact_predicates_exact_constructions_kernel`.

This choice stems from the recurring computation of the intersection of moving elements.
In 3D, the key geometric operation is the intersection of four shifting 3D (supporting) planes,
answering the question: when and where will these four planes intersect in a single point.
The position and time correspond to a potential event involving these four facets.

For the computation to be meaningful, these planes must be normalized. This inevitably introduces
square root computations.

However, due to the mandatory perturbation mechanisms described in \ref Straight_skeleton_3GeneralPosition,
the use of a traits providing exact square root computations is not recommended: a (tiny) numerical
error in the normalization of planes is anyway indistinguishable from the perturbation.
Exact constructions are required to ensure that all vertices lay exactly on the supporting planes
of their respective incident facets.
